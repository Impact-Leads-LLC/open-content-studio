# Post Types: Templates, Formulas and Examples

Read this before writing any post. Eight types. Each has a job, a skeleton and rules. Some have a short example.

**About the examples:** each one is written for a made-up business (a bookkeeping firm or a fitness studio) with made-up facts, and most are shorter than the real word ranges. They show structure only. Never reuse their facts, numbers, keywords or wording, and never take the voice from them. The voice comes from the brand documents.

---

## Hook formulas (the first line of any post)

The first line is a door, never a summary. Use one of these:

1. **Contrarian claim:** "Most small businesses don't need a monthly meeting with their bookkeeper."
2. **Specific number or name:** "One change to our intro offer cut first-month dropouts from one in three to one in ten."
3. **Curiosity gap:** "A client asked me one question last week that changed how she prices every job."
4. **Quoted line or dialogue:** "I'll start once I've lost ten pounds on my own."
5. **Reader callout:** "If you dread opening your bank app at the end of the month, read this."
6. **Time-stamped story open:** "In 2019 I lost my two biggest clients in the same week."

If the review standard sets its own test for the first line (for example, a number and an outcome in the reader's own words), pass that test first. The formulas combine with it: a number inside a reader callout, an outcome inside a quoted line.

Test: would a 12-year-old keep reading after line one? If not, rewrite the hook.

## 1. Short hook post (~40% of the feed)

**Job:** catch a different slice of the feed. Pattern interrupt. Often the caption on a carousel or video.

**Skeleton:** one line, or two or three very short lines. No setup. No ask, or a single pointer ("swipe", "video below").

**Rules:** 35 words or fewer. One sharp idea. No hashtag padding. At most one emoji.

**Examples (structure only, made-up businesses):**
> Members who book a Monday class are twice as likely to still be training in June.

> I asked 40 business owners what they hate most about bookkeeping. 31 gave the same answer.

> Send your receipts every Friday and your tax bill stops being a surprise.

## 2. Belief / teaching, no offer (~21%)

**Job:** build authority and trust by teaching one thing and asking for nothing.

**Skeleton:**
1. Hook (a claim or a reader callout)
2. The common mistake or belief
3. The reframe: move the problem to a level the reader can actually solve
4. The reasoning, in short lines
5. One clean takeaway. No ask.

**Rules:** 120 to 280 words. One idea, not five. End on the takeaway and resist adding an ask. If the idea came from a guest, name the guest in the first few lines.

**Example (structure only, a made-up bookkeeping firm):**
> Most small business owners look at their numbers once a year, in April.
>
> By then the year is over, and the tax bill is whatever it is.
>
> A monthly check fixes most of that, and it takes about 15 minutes.
>
> On the first Monday of each month, look at the cash in your account, the money clients owe you and the bills due in the next 30 days.
>
> If a client is slow to pay, you see it in week three, while a phone call still works.
>
> If you've had a big month, you can move tax money aside while it's still in the account.
>
> Fifteen minutes a month will tell you more than one long meeting in April.

## 3. Workshop / live invite (~16%)

**Job:** fill a live session or push its replay.

**Skeleton:**
1. Hook (the problem the session solves)
2. What they'll walk away with (two to four concrete things, never vague benefits)
3. The logistics: one date, one time
4. One ask (a registration link or a comment keyword)

**Rules:** write this type only when the brief or the app gives the real session details. Concrete takeaways only. No seat scarcity unless the cap is real. One ask.

**Example (structure only, a made-up fitness studio):**
> If you've tried to start exercising three times this year and stopped each time, this is for you.
>
> On Saturday we're running a free one-hour session at the studio for complete beginners.
>
> You'll leave with:
>
> - A 20-minute routine you can do at home with no equipment
> - A simple way to tell if you're pushing too hard
> - A plan for your first four weeks
>
> Saturday 14 March, 10am. There are 12 places, because that's how many people fit on the floor.
>

## 4. Personal / lifestyle, no offer (~8%, highest engagement)

**Job:** make the feed human. These earn the attention the offer posts spend.

**Skeleton:**
1. A real moment, stated plainly (travel, family, a decision, a small ritual)
2. What it meant or what it cost
3. A loose tie to a business or life truth, kept light
4. No ask. Maybe one emoji.

**Rules:** write this type only when the excerpt has the owner describing a real moment from their own life. Use their details and add none. No bait. The business tie is a whisper, never a pivot to selling.

## 5. Personal story + offer (~6%, the flagship)

**Job:** the hero post. Earn trust with a real story, then make one soft offer.

**Skeleton:**
1. Hook: a quoted line or a time-stamped open
2. The backstory and the situation (short lines, white space)
3. The turn: the moment that changes how the story reads
4. The lesson, stated plainly
5. Bridge to the reader: who else is in that same spot
6. One ask: comment a keyword for the free resource

**Rules:** 250 to 450 words. Real numbers. The story and the lesson must both come from the owner's own words in the source, and the lesson must be earned by the story, never bolted on. A P.S. is optional, to restate the free thing.

**Example (structure only, a made-up fitness studio, shortened):**
> "I'll start once I've lost ten pounds on my own."
>
> A woman told me that at our front desk in 2021. She left, and I didn't see her again for two years.
>
> When she came back, she said she'd tried to get in shape four times on her own. Each time she stopped after a few weeks.
>
> That bothered me, because our intro offer was built for people who already felt fit enough to walk into a group class.
>
> So we changed it. New members now start with three one-to-one sessions at an easy pace, then join a group.
>
> In the first year, the share of new members who quit in their first month fell from about one in three to about one in ten.
>
> What I took from it: people wait until they feel ready, so the first step has to be small enough to take before they do.
>
> If you've been waiting to feel fit enough to start, I wrote down exactly what those first three sessions look like.
>
> Comment START and I'll send it to you.

## 6. Teaching + offer (~5%)

**Job:** teach something genuinely useful, then offer the deeper free resource.

**Skeleton:**
1. Hook
2. A real, usable teaching: give away something that works
3. The honest limit: where this gets the reader, and where it stops
4. One ask to the free resource that goes deeper

**Rules:** the teaching in the post must stand on its own. The ask offers more; it never withholds the actual answer. The free resource and its keyword must exist in the brief.

**Example (structure only, a made-up bookkeeping firm):**
> Here's a check you can run on your business this week.
>
> Open last month's bank statement and highlight every payment that repeats each month.
>
> Then ask one question about each one: would you sign up for this today?
>
> Most owners who try this find two or three subscriptions they had forgotten about.
>
> That gets you some money back each month.
>
> It won't tell you whether your prices cover your real costs, and that's where most small businesses lose far more.
>
> I made a one-page sheet that walks you through that second check.
>
> Comment MARGIN and I'll send it over.

## 7. Client case study (~2%, highest-engagement business post)

**Job:** show the owner's work paying off for a real client.

**Skeleton (plain-text labels, each on its own line ending in a colon):**
1. **Headline:** How [client] went from [before] to [after]
2. **The problem:** where they were, honestly. Two to four dash lines.
3. **What we did:** what changed. Two to four dash lines.
4. **The result:** the specific outcome, with numbers.
5. **What I respect about them:** the human part.
6. One ask.

**Rules:** use only a client result from the brand brief's proof, or one the owner describes in the excerpt. Names, numbers and conditions must match the source exactly; use a client's name only if the source names them. If there is no real result, pick another type. The "what I respect" part drives the comments, and it is the owner's opinion, so it must come from what the owner says in the source. If the source doesn't give it, leave that part out and flag it in the notes for the owner to add.

**Example (structure only, a made-up bookkeeping firm and client):**
> How a two-person landscaping company cut its month-end close from 9 days to 2
>
> The problem:
> - Receipts lived in two trucks and three email inboxes
> - Invoices went out late, so cash came in late
>
> What we did:
> - Set up one shared inbox for every receipt
> - Moved invoicing to the day each job finished
>
> The result:
> - Month-end close went from 9 days to 2
> - Clients paid an average of 11 days sooner
>
> What I respect about them: they changed a habit they'd had for twelve years, and they did it in their busiest season.
>
> If your month-end takes more than a week, comment CLOSE and I'll send you the checklist we used.

## 8. Launch / scarcity (~2%, real launches only)

**Job:** push during a genuine, capped launch.

**Skeleton:**
1. Hook: what's open and why now
2. What's inside, stated plainly
3. The real constraint (the cap or the close date), stated once and calmly
4. One ask

**Rules:** write this type only when the brief or the app names a real launch with its real cap or close date. No countdown drama and no "8 spots left" unless it is literally true. Calm authority: present the case and let the reader decide.
